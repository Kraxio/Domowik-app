Faded receipt? How to store receipts so they don't disappear

A thermal receipt can become unreadable within a few months — long before the warranty ends. Here is what harms it, how to store it and how to keep proof of purchase for years.

Why receipts fade

Most receipts are printed on thermal paper. There is no ink on it — the image is created by heating a chemical layer that coats the paper. That is why printing is fast and quiet, but also why it is short-lived: the same layer the printer head once heated later reacts to other factors and gradually darkens or disappears.

What harms a receipt the most:

  • Heat — a receipt left in a hot car can fade within a few days.
  • Sunlight — long exposure to the sun speeds up yellowing of the whole slip.
  • Friction — rubbing against other objects wears off the layer, which is why a receipt in a wallet quickly becomes unreadable.
  • Contact with film and plastic — some plastics, especially PVC document sleeves, react with the thermal layer.
  • Grease and chemicals — traces of hand cream, sanitiser or greasy fingers can locally blacken or erase the print.

In practice this means a receipt for a washing machine or a laptop can become unreadable long before the warranty ends. And a faded receipt usually cannot be recovered — the content is gone for good.

How to store paper receipts

If you want to keep the paper itself, the point is to cut it off from everything that speeds up fading. The rule is simple: dark, cool, dry and separate.

  • A paper envelope or folder — paper does not react with the thermal layer, unlike PVC sleeves.
  • Away from heat and sun — a drawer or binder in a room, not a windowsill or a car glovebox.
  • Separately from other receipts — so the print doesn't transfer and rub off against neighbouring slips.
  • Not in a wallet — constant friction and body heat are among the fastest ways to wear off the print.

This helps, but no paper storage lasts forever. That is why the safest solution is a digital copy — and it's best to make it straight away.

Make a copy right after the purchase

The best moment for a copy is right after buying — before the receipt ends up in a pocket and before it starts to age. It is the only moment when you can be sure the print is fully legible.

You have several options, from the most convenient:

  • A phone photo — the fastest and always at hand. Place the receipt on a flat background, in good light, with no hand shadow, and focus on the date, amount and shop name. Photograph a long receipt in two parts.
  • A scan — if you have a scanner or a scanning app, it gives the clearest file.
  • A photocopy on plain paper — a laser print is far more durable than the thermal original, so the copy will outlast the receipt.

The key is for the copy to be linked to a specific thing, rather than floating loose in your phone gallery. A year later, that link is the hardest part to reconstruct. We cover the whole habit of organising proof of purchase in our guide on storing receipts and warranties.

What to do when a receipt has already faded

The question that comes up most often first: how do I read a faded receipt? If the print is still partly visible, gentle, careful warming of the slip (a hairdryer from a distance, or a warm lamp) can sometimes darken the thermal layer just enough to take a photo. It is unreliable, though, and once the print has disappeared completely the content usually cannot be recovered.

But even then the situation is rarely hopeless. A receipt is only one of several possible proofs of purchase — it's worth checking the others one by one:

  • A card payment confirmation or bank statement — the date and amount point to a purchase at a given shop.
  • An e-receipt or email from the shop — if you gave your address at checkout, or bought online.
  • An invoice — if one was issued, it is usually more durable than a thermal receipt.
  • A shop account or retailer app — some sellers keep a purchase history.

What exactly a seller accepts, and on what terms, varies between shops and countries — it's best to ask the seller directly. On how to keep track of warranty and complaint deadlines, we have a separate guide on warranties and deadlines. Either way, the simplest thing is to secure the proof up front — a single photo on the day of purchase solves this problem before it appears.

Frequently asked questions

Why do receipts fade so quickly?

Because most receipts are printed on thermal paper, which has no ink — the image is formed by heating a chemical layer. That layer later reacts to heat, light, friction and contact with plastic or grease, so the print yellows and disappears, sometimes within a few months.

Can a faded receipt be recovered?

Usually not the print itself — once the thermal layer fades, the content is gone for good. That is why a copy made earlier matters: a phone photo taken on the day of purchase captures the data before the receipt ages. If there is no copy, you are left looking for other proof of purchase.

What is the best way to store a receipt so it doesn't fade?

In a dark, cool and dry place, separately from other documents, ideally in a paper envelope. Avoid wallets (friction and heat) and PVC sleeves, which react with the thermal layer. The safest option, though, is to make a digital copy right after the purchase.

Will a shop accept a complaint with a faded receipt?

It depends on the shop, but a faded receipt alone can be a problem if the date, amount or seller name can no longer be read. That is why it helps to have a legible copy made earlier, and to add other proof of purchase as backup: a card payment confirmation, a bank statement, an e-receipt or an invoice. What exactly a seller accepts is best confirmed directly.

How do I read a faded receipt?

If the print is still partly visible, gentle, careful warming of the receipt (for example a hairdryer from a distance or a warm lamp) can sometimes darken the thermal layer just enough to take a photo. This only works with partly legible print and is unreliable. Once the content has disappeared completely, the print usually cannot be read anymore — you are left with other proof of purchase (card payment, statement, e-receipt, invoice). That is why it is best to take a clear photo right after the purchase, before the receipt ages.
